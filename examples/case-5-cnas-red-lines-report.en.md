## I. Event Background (June 2026)

In June 2026, the U.S. national security think tank Center for a New American Security (CNAS) published *Red Lines: Understanding the National Security Risks of China's Advanced AI*, authored by Daniel Remler, Senior Fellow in CNAS's Technology and National Security program.

The report's core claim (first sentence of the Executive Summary):

> "Chinese advanced AI systems pose a **serious and growing threat** to U.S. national security."

The report proposes a "three domains, two vectors" framework (kinetic / cognitive / economic-technological × state instrumentalization / proliferation and dependency), assesses seven Chinese AI developers (Alibaba, ByteDance, DeepSeek, MiniMax, Moonshot, Tencent, and Zhipu), and offers six policy recommendations: 72-hour risk assessments, CISA alerts, security testing guidelines, classified adversarial testing, allied coordination, and semiannual reports.

The report also criticizes the "Western AI community's fixation on U.S. frontier systems" for creating an "analytical vacuum," and argues for understanding risk in "absolute terms" rather than by "relative rankings."

## IV. Material Factual Flaws (A-Anchored Label Inventory)

Per the Discourse Game Formula — "when criticizing others, stand on verifiable material facts" — the following are the report's own **publicly verifiable textual factual flaws**, each an A-anchored label checkable by any independent reader:

### 4.1 The "seven developers" list contradicts itself

- Executive Summary (p. 1): "China's seven leading AI developers **(Alibaba, Baidu, DeepSeek, MiniMax, Moonshot, Tencent, and Zhipu)**" — includes **Baidu**, no ByteDance;
- Main text, Risk Assessment (p. 6): "These measures identify seven Chinese developers as ecosystem leaders: **Alibaba, ByteDance, DeepSeek, MiniMax, Moonshot, Tencent, and Zhipu**" — includes **ByteDance**, no **Baidu**.

**The "seven developers" list — the very foundation of the entire analysis — is two different lists in the opening and the main text.** A report that cannot even keep its "research subjects" self-consistent casts doubt on the rigor of its "absolute capability assessment."

### 4.2 The Zhipu Entity List date contradicts itself

- Main text (p. 7): "land it on the U.S. Department of Commerce Entity List in **January 2025**";
- Conclusion (p. 16): "The U.S. government has not acted against any Chinese AI developer since adding Zhipu to the Entity List in **2024**".

The same fact is dated January 2025 in one place and 2024 in another. And the conclusion uses precisely this date to argue that "the U.S. government has not acted against any Chinese AI developer since 2024" — **the date error directly undermines the force of its policy criticism.**

### 4.3 The "absolute capability" framework admits it is not yet built

Conclusion chapter, verbatim: "The current evaluation infrastructure...**does not yet fully support this approach**."

The report uses a framework it admits is not yet built to support a set of already very definite policy conclusions.

### 4.4 The national double standard between "legitimate" and "adversarial" distillation

- Main text (p. 8): "Distillation—here, the **legitimate practice** of training a smaller model on a developer's own larger system";
- Introduction (p. 2): "the CCP seeks advantage...through **adversarial distillation** (training on the outputs of superior U.S. models...)".

The same technical practice is "legitimate practice" when done by U.S. companies and "adversarial" when done by Chinese companies. The report gives no technical criterion distinguishing the two; the only distinguishing criterion is **nationality**.

### 4.5 The report's own counterexample negates its own causal attribution

Main text (p. 10): "open-weight Chinese models can be fine-tuned...**a vulnerability shared by U.S. open-weight models**."

If the vulnerability is "shared by U.S. and Chinese models," then the causal chain attributing the vulnerability to "CCP political attributes" is negated by the report's own evidence.
